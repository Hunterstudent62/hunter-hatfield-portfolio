Calculus III and the Mathematics of Insect Flight 
Mathematics primarily concerns itself with its own terminology and laws, but it is 
integral to our understanding of physics. New applications of previously theoretical 
mathematical concepts continue to arise, especially in scientific fields. Biological systems are 
complex and cross-disciplinary, but physics and applied mathematics are often hidden inside real 
biological systems, such as animal motion. Insect flight is incredibly fascinating, especially the 
advanced aerial locomotion of the order Diptera, commonly known as the true flies. Flies have 
some of the greatest aerial mobility among all animals, being able to hover, turn, and maneuver 
in ways that look simple but are very physically complicated. I chose this topic for my paper 
because I am passionate about the intersection of my major, Computer Science, and wildlife, 
ecology, and biological research. The ways in which insect flight connects to Calculus III include 
vector-valued functions, acceleration, vector fields, and forces acting from multiple directions, 
such as inertia, gravity, and propulsion. Essentially, insect flight is the perfect example of 
Calculus III concepts used to describe real motion and airflow in living organisms. 
Insect flight is more complicated than it may appear at first and is extensively studied by 
biologists, physicists, and engineers alike. “The flight of insects has fascinated physicists and 
biologists for more than a century” (Sane, 2003, p. 4191). Some of the older, simpler airplane 
concepts were not enough to explain flapping flight because insect wings do not move in a linear 
way but as a combination of multiple vectors, rotation, and interaction with moving air. New 
tools such as high-speed video, mechanical wing models, and computational models make insect 
flight easier to study than ever before. These tools often depend on measuring position, direction, 
speed, acceleration, and force.

Calculus III uses vector-valued functions like r(t) to describe the position of something 
moving through 3D space. A fly’s body position, or even a single point on the surface of a fly’s 
wing, can be thought of as a position vector changing over time. The derivative r’(t) gives 
velocity, meaning the direction and speed at that specific moment, just as its second derivative 
gives acceleration. Flies can perform very complex aerial maneuvers, such as flipping upside 
down midair and landing on the ceiling. This involves rapid acceleration in order to change 
upward motion and body orientation in a fraction of a second. A successful inverted landing 
involves “an initial upward acceleration followed by rapid body rotation and leg extension” (Liu 
et al., 2019). 
V ectors have not only direction but also magnitude, and this is true for forces such as lift, 
drag, gravity, and propulsion. Flies must produce enough upward force to stay in the air while 
also controlling rotational motion for steering and complex movements forward, backward, and 
sideways. A fly’s wings do not simply push air downward but rotate and interact with air left 
behind by previous wingbeats. Insect flight depends on “delayed stall, rotational circulation, and 
wake capture” (Dickinson et al., 1999, p. 1954). The wing can maintain useful airflow and lift 
during part of the wing stroke instead of immediately losing lift like a simple fixed wing, such as 
an airplane wing, might. When the wing turns around at the end of a stroke, that rotation and the 
remaining moving air can help produce extra force. In Calculus III terms, these forces can be 
described using vectors, while a dot product could measure how much of a force acts in a 
specific direction, such as upward lift or forward motion. 
The rotation of the fly’s wings is the key to rapid angular change during flight and 
redirects forces quickly. Insects can “modulate the direction and magnitude of flight forces” 
(Dickinson et al., 1999, p. 1954). When a fly changes the angle and motion of its wings, it

changes the aerodynamic force vectors acting on its body. If the net force changes, the fly’s 
acceleration changes. This connects to the idea of a space curve in Calculus III, where the 
position of a moving point can be described by a vector-valued function over time. A fly’s 
motion through the air can be thought of as a point tracing a curved path through three-
dimensional space, but the actual motion is more complicated because that path is constantly 
affected by several changing force vectors, including lift, drag, gravity, and propulsion. In a 
simulation of fly movement, a computer model would need to account for position, velocity, 
acceleration, wing angle, and changing force vectors over time. 
A vector field is a useful way to imagine the medium the fly is pushing and moving 
through: the air. In a vector field, each point in space has a vector showing the direction as well 
as the speed of flow at that location. A fly’s wing creates complicated airflow because of the 
redistribution of the air and the altering of the vectors of existing particles. A leading-edge vortex 
“greatly enhances the forces generated by the wing” (Sane, 2003, p. 4191). A vortex is a spinning 
airflow that can help increase the aerodynamic force produced by the wing. This connects to 
Calculus III because vector fields describe changing flow through space, while flux describes 
how much flow passes across a surface. Full fluid dynamics is more advanced than what I have 
learned so far, but Calculus III provides the basic language for describing direction, flow, and 
force. 
In our ordinary lives, flies are more often seen as an annoyance and are not really given 
more than a glancing thought, but flies are a vital part of the ecosystem as pollinators, 
decomposers, and prey animals. Their flight affects how they find food, escape predators, locate 
mates, and spread through habitats. A fly’s ability to maneuver itself is important to 
understanding its biology and also to understanding how flies can have such a large effect on the

ecosystem. Outside of biological fields, a fly’s movement is useful for engineering as it can be 
used to inspire new and more complex models of flight for technologies like drones and other 
aerial devices. One direct example of this kind of biological inspiration is the Harvard RoboBee 
project. Researchers created an insect-scale flying robot modeled partly after fly biology, with 
small flapping wings and a control system designed to manage the instability of flight at such a 
small size (Ma et al., 2013). Possible applications for this kind of work include environmental 
monitoring, search-and-rescue, and even crop pollination, which connects insect flight research 
back to both ecology and technology. As a Computer Science student with an interest in biology 
and entomology, this topic shows how math can support models, simulations, and animal-related 
technology. 
This topic helped me personally connect how Calculus concepts are more than just 
abstract formulas and have real-life applications to physics and living movement. V ector-valued 
functions, velocity, and acceleration come together to describe something that seems exceedingly 
complicated when viewed as a whole. Mathematics allows complex problems to be studied 
quantitatively, which is especially useful when studying animal motion. This paper helped me

homework problem. This topic relates to my future interest in Computer Science, such as 
ecological data and simulation software, and I believe it prepares me to think more critically 
about the application of mathematics. 
Fly flight is a real-world example of Calculus III concepts being applied. V ector-valued 
functions describe the position of the fly itself, derivatives describe the fly’s velocity and 
acceleration, vectors describe the individual acting forces on the fly, and vector fields relate to 
the airflow around the organism. Both physical and mathematical models are used to understand
